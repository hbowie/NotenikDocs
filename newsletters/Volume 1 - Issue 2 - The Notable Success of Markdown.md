Title:  Volume 1, Issue 2 - The Notable Success of Markdown

Seq:    1.3

Level:  3

Body:

[Markdown](https://daringfireball.net/projects/markdown/) seems to be having a bit of a moment. 

Anil Dash recently explained "[How Markdown Took Over The World](https://www.anildash.com/2026/01/09/how-markdown-took-over-the-world/)."

And creator John Gruber recently noted the appearance of "[The Fourth Horseman of the File-Format-Hegemony Apocalypse](https://daringfireball.net/linked/2026/08/21/fourth-horseman-of-the-apocalypse-markdow)," pointing out that the Microsoft iPhone app for OneDrive now offers a button to create a new Markdown file, right in line with its long-standing options to create new Word, Excel and PowerPoint docs. 

Much of the recent upswing in interest is based on Markdown having become the [lingua franca for the LLMs](https://www.craftmarkdown.com/why-llms-love-markdown) powering all of the current AI surge – a use case that Gruber couldn't have possibly predicted when he initially fashioned his deceptively simple little markup language back in 2004.  

There is a through line to all of this, though. 

Albert Einstein [didn't exactly say](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2011/05/13/einstein-simple/) "Everything should be made as simple as possible, but not simpler," but people wish he had, and Markdown is a perfect example of the application of this adage. 

The scale of the accomplishment here should not be underestimated. For all practical purposes, modern text production begins with the humble typewriter. And while the text so produced is indeed simple, it is a little *too* simple, without even the ability to use italics for emphasis. 

Then we move on to word processor territory, and here text production ends up being not nearly simple enough, with a wild profusion of choices in typeface, font size, color – effects that initially seem fun and liberating but in the end just prove grating. 

Then we move on to HTML and, again, for the purposes of the writer, and even sometimes for the reader, it's not simple enough, carrying forward many of the same issues found in word processing software. 

And then lightweight markup languages came along, which offered hope for the humble author just trying to string words together in order to express ideas. 

But even then, languages like Textile were still not simple enough, with an excess of options for generating all sorts of HTML tags having little to do with words or ideas. 

And so Gruber trod a careful path between these two extremes, being simple enough to be easily written and read and parsed, while steadfastly resisting the siren song of additional complexity.
